A data story about Australia’s toy box

The Great Toy Reroute

Australia buys more toys than ever — so why are fewer of them coming from China?

Colours used on every chart: China Vietnam Japan everyone else

1 · The toy box

Australia now imports A$5.6 billion of toys a year

In 2006 Australia imported A$2.07 billion worth of toys, games and sporting goods.1 By 2025 the bill had reached A$5.63 billion — 2.7 times as much. The steepest climb came between 2019 and 2022, when it rose from A$3.54 billion to A$5.11 billion.

These are dollar values, not counts of toys, and they are not adjusted for inflation.

Why can households afford all these toys?

Recreation — toys, games, sport, hobbies, holidays and entertainment — has taken a remarkably steady slice of the household budget: between 11.8% and 13.2% of spending in every ABS Household Expenditure Survey from 1984 to 2015–16. In dollars, the average household’s weekly recreation spend went from A$43.13 to A$171.85 over those 32 years.

What changed is what that slice buys. From 1993–94 to 2015–16, prices for recreation and culture rose 28.5%, while prices across the whole economy rose about 76%.2 Compared with everything else, recreation got roughly 27% cheaper — so the same share of the budget stretches a lot further.

2 · One country’s toy box

For twenty years, two in every three toy dollars went to China

Older Australians may remember toys stamped “Made in Japan” or “Made in England”. That era is real, but its records are patchy: consistent trade figures for this category only start in 2006, so it stays a story here rather than a chart.

From 2006 the picture is simple. China supplied 65.5% of Australia’s toy imports by value in 2006, climbed to a peak of 76.0% in 2020, and still supplied 68.3% in 2025.

3 · The reroute

The grip is loosening — and Vietnam is the story

China’s share peaked in 2020 and has slipped back since. Over the same years Vietnam surged. In 2006 it supplied 0.3% of Australia’s toy imports and ranked 17th. In 2024 it supplied 4.8%. One year later it supplied 7.2% — enough to overtake the United States for second place.

Vietnam is not alone. Together, five South-East Asian countries — Vietnam, Thailand, Indonesia, Malaysia and Singapore — went from 3.2% of the market in 2006 to 10.7% in 2025.

Japan is the quieter comeback: 1.1% in 2022, 2.8% in 2025. The trade figures can’t see inside the category, but the timing fits the boom in collectible trading cards and figures — a reminder that “toys” here also means games and collections.

4 · Where and when they land

Toys don’t arrive where — or when — you’d think

Where: ports, not playrooms

This part of the data records the state or territory where imports were unloaded, not where the toys end up. It is a map of ports and warehouses.3 In the 2024–25 financial year, Victoria took in 45% of Australia’s toy imports by value and New South Wales 37%. Victoria alone received 58% of the toys from Vietnam.

The ACT recorded just A$13 thousand and Tasmania A$1.07 million. That isn’t because few children live there: the ACT has no seaport at all, and goods bound for both are mostly unloaded in other states first.

The charts in this part use financial years (July to June). Every other chart on the page uses calendar years.

When: October, not December

Toy imports have a strong yearly rhythm. Using every complete year in the monthly data (2023–2025), an average October brings in 148.5% of an average month’s imports, and an average March just 72.0% — a 2.1-fold swing.

Shoppers have a rhythm too, but a different one. Discretionary household spending — everything from toys to takeaway to travel — peaks in December at 121.2% of an average month and bottoms out in February at 88.2%. By December, when spending peaks, toy imports have already fallen back to 93.5%.

Put the two rhythms on one chart and they draw a loop rather than a line. That loop is the two-month lag: toys land in spring and wait in warehouses and on shelves for the Christmas rush. Neither dataset could show this on its own.5

So…

Why are fewer of our toys coming from China?

Strictly speaking, more toys than ever still come from China: A$3.84 billion worth in 2025, nearly three times the 2006 figure. What has fallen since 2020 is China’s share. The new growth is going elsewhere, most of all to Vietnam, whose toy exports to Australia rose from A$5 million to A$403 million in twenty years.

The trade figures can’t say why. But the pattern fits toy makers spreading production across more countries in South-East Asia. Whichever factory they come from, the toys still land in Melbourne and Sydney in October, ready for December.

Notes on the data

  1. “Toys” means prams, toys, games and sporting goods. The trade figures use the United Nations category SITC 894 (“baby carriages, toys, games and sporting goods”), which can’t be split any finer at this level. A toys-only series exists in UN Comtrade (HS 9503) but is not used here.
  2. Price figures. The 28.5% rise is the ABS consumer price index for recreation and culture, 80.7 in 1993–94 → 103.7 in 2015–16, as published with the Household Expenditure Survey tables. The all-groups figure is the ABS consumer price index for all goods and services, averaged over the same two financial years: 42.66 → 75.21 on the current base (September 2025 = 100), a rise of 76.3%.
  3. States are where goods were unloaded, not where people live. The ACT and Tasmania look tiny because their goods mostly arrive through other states. These figures should never be read per person or per child. Port locations come from each port’s Wikipedia article.
  4. The UK blip in 2015 and 2016. The United Kingdom’s share jumped to 4.2% and 4.6% in those two calendar years, then fell back to about 1%. A short spike like this is almost certainly how a distributor routed or recorded goods, not a burst of British-made toys, so no argument here rests on it.
  5. The lag chart compares toy imports with discretionary household spending, not toy spending. The ABS Monthly Household Spending Indicator has no toy category. Its seasonal index uses complete calendar years 2013–2025; the toy-import index uses 2023–2025, the only complete years in the monthly trade data.
  6. Calendar and financial years are never mixed on one axis. Country totals are calendar years (2006–2025); state figures are financial years (2015–16 to 2024–25), and every chart says which it uses.
  7. 2026 is a partial year. Monthly figures end in August 2026, so 2026 is never shown as a full year.
  8. Maps. “Rest of world” is a mix of many countries with no single location, so it is left off every map. Values are in Australian dollars and are not adjusted for inflation.
